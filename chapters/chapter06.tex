Chapters 4 and 5 demonstrated the feasibility and thermal advantages of implementing the proposed flatwise-wound transformer in a small-scale, 1~kW prototype through both finite element simulations and experimental evaluations. As theorized in Chapter 3 and validated in the preceding chapters, the flatwise winding orientation establishes parallel thermal conduction paths that significantly lower the intrinsic thermal resistance of the transformer compared to conventional edgewise windings. To further evaluate the practical viability of the proposed architecture for broader applications, this chapter explores the scalability of the flatwise-wound transformer in terms of the benefit it can provide compared to its conventional counterpart.

Specifically, this scalability analysis is divided into two main investigations:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Geometric Scalability:} Evaluating the spatial effect of varying the core window aspect ratio (from low-profile to high-profile geometries) on the electromagnetic and thermal performance while maintaining a constant magnetic flux density $B_{\mathrm{m}}$, current density $J$, and magnetizing inductance $L_{\mathrm{m}}$.
    \item \textbf{Power Scalability:} Evaluating the suitability of the proposed winding structure as the operating power is scaled from 1~kW up to 10~kW and 100~kW using the Area-Product ($AP$) sizing method, while maintaining constant magnetic flux density $B_{\mathrm{m}}$, current density $J$, window occupancy ratio $K_\mathrm{u}$, and magnetizing inductance $L_{\mathrm{m}}$.
\end{enumerate}
Once the scalability potential of the flatwise-wound transformer is established in these two aspects, then theoretically any kind of geometry and power rating would be feasible for the flatwise-wound transformer, allowing for flexible adaptation across diverse high-power-density converter constraints.

\section{Power Scalability}
While the 1~kW prototype and geometric sweep validate the fundamental heat extraction mechanism of the flatwise winding structure, High-Frequency Transformers (HFTs) deployed in modular SSTs and high-power DC--DC converters typically operate at tens to hundreds of kilowatts. Therefore, a power scalability analysis is conducted to evaluate the electromagnetic and thermal performance of the proposed transformer architecture as the rated power is scaled from 1~kW to 10~kW and 100~kW.

\subsection{Electrical Scaling Guidelines}
To perform a rigorous and fair comparison across different power levels, the electrical parameters of the CLLC resonant converter are scaled such that the underlying circuit dynamics and resonant tank characteristics remain consistent. Specifically, as the output power $P_{\mathrm{o}}$ is increased by a factor of 10 (to 10~kW) and 100 (to 100~kW), both the input and output voltages $V_{\mathrm{in}}, V_{\mathrm{o}}$ and the input and output currents $I_{\mathrm{in}}, I_{\mathrm{o}}$ are scaled equally. 

By scaling voltage and current at the same rate as had been explained in Chapter \ref{sec:established}, the effective load resistance $R_{\mathrm{o}} = V_{\mathrm{o}}/I_{\mathrm{o}}$ and the equivalent AC resistance derived via FHA given by Eq. \ref{eq:Rac}
remain constant across all power levels. Consequently, the quality factor $Q$, inductance ratio $k = 10.0$, voltage gain curve $M$, and resonant tank components $C_{\mathrm{r1}} = C_{\mathrm{r2}} = 1.4~\upmu\text{F}$, $L_{\mathrm{r1}} = L_{\mathrm{r2}} = 20.1~\upmu\text{H}$, and $L_{\mathrm{m}} = 200~\upmu\text{H}$ remain identical for the 1~kW, 10~kW, and 100~kW designs. The electrical specifications across the three power ratings are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:power_electrical_specs}. More insight behind this choice of parameters is explained in Chapter \ref{sec:established} and sample working simulations at 1 kW and 100 kW are shown in the Appendix: \nameref{app:simulation}.

\subsection{Transformer Power Scaling with the $AP$ Method} \label{chap:AP}
To scale the physical geometry of the baseline square-window transformers, which were denoted as Tr2E and Tr2F in the previous section, the Area-Product ($AP$) method \cite{Kimura2015, Colonel2004} is utilized to examine the thermal performance of the transformers at 10 kW and 100 kW operation. From here, the 1 kW edgewise and flatwise-wound transformers are denoted as TrE1 and TrF1, the 10~kW edgewise and flatwise-wound as TrE10 and TrF10 respectively, and the 100~kW edgewise and flatwise-wound as TrE100 and TrF100 respectively. The $AP$ relates the physical window area $A_{\mathrm{w}}$ and magnetic core cross-sectional area $A_{\mathrm{c}}$ (indicated by Eq. \ref{eq:AP_1}) to the electrical and magnetic constraints of the converter (indicated by Eq. \ref{eq:AP_2}, Eq. \ref{eq:AP_3}, or Eq. \ref{eq:AP_4}), with an example reference figure in Figure \ref{fig:AP}. A more detailed explanation is provided in the Appendix: \nameref{app:downsizing}.
\begin{align}
    AP &= A_{\mathrm{w}} \cdot A_{\mathrm{c}} \label{eq:AP_1} \\
    &= \frac{L_{\mathrm{m}}I_{\mathrm{p,pk}}(I_{\mathrm{p,rms}} + I_{\mathrm{s,rms}})}{B_{\mathrm{m}}J_{\mathrm{m}}K_{\mathrm{u}}} \label{eq:AP_2} \\
    &= \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}(I_{\mathrm{p,rms}} + I_{\mathrm{s,rms}})}{K_{\mathrm{f}} f_{\mathrm{sw}} B_{\mathrm{m}} J_{\mathrm{m}} K_{\mathrm{u}}} \label{eq:AP_3}\\
    &= \frac{2 P_{\mathrm{o}}}{K_\mathrm{f} f_{\mathrm{sw}} B_{\mathrm{m}} J_{\mathrm{m}} K_{\mathrm{u}}} \label{eq:AP_4} 
\end{align}
where $K_{\mathrm{f}} = 4$ is the waveform coefficient for square-wave voltage excitation ($n = 1$, $D = 0.5$), $B_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the maximum core flux density, $J_{\mathrm{m}}$ is the current density, and $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ is the window utilization factor.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width = 0.7\columnwidth]{figures_chapter6/ap.pdf}
  \caption{EE-core with $A_\mathrm{w}$ and $A_\mathrm{c}$ indicated.}
    \label{fig:AP}
\end{figure}

In this scaling procedure, $L_{\mathrm{m}}$, $B_{\mathrm{m}}$, $J_{\mathrm{m}}$, $K_{\mathrm{u}}$, and the number of turns $N_{\mathrm{p}}:N_{\mathrm{s}} = 8:8$ are held constant across all power levels. Because Eq. \ref{eq:AP_3} suits the scaling procedure the most given the scaling of voltage and current with the aforementioned parameters held constant, separating the area product into its constituent components yields Eq. \ref{eq:Ac} and Eq. \ref{eq:Ac_Aw} for $A_\mathrm{c}$ and $A_\mathrm{w}$ respectively. An inequality is added to Eq. \ref{eq:Ac} to avoid saturation.
\begin{align}
    A_{\mathrm{c}} &\ge \frac{V_{\mathrm{in}}}{4 f_{\mathrm{sw}} N_{\mathrm{p}} B_{\mathrm{m}}}, \label{eq:Ac} \\
    A_{\mathrm{w}} &= \frac{N_{\mathrm{p}}(I_{\mathrm{p,rms}} + I_{\mathrm{s,rms}})}{J_{\mathrm{m}} K_{\mathrm{u}}}. \label{eq:Ac_Aw}
\end{align}
Thus, as $V_\mathrm{in}$ and $V_\mathrm{o}$ scale, from Eq. \ref{eq:Ac}, $A_{\mathrm{c}} \propto V_{\mathrm{in}}$. On the other hand, as $I_\mathrm{in}$ and $I_\mathrm{o}$ scale, from Eq. \ref{eq:Ac_Aw}, $A_{\mathrm{w}} \propto I$. Here, $I_\mathrm{p}$ and $I_\mathrm{s}$ are also $I_\mathrm{in}$ and $I_\mathrm{o}$ respectively, derived from the voltage and power specifications.

One-dimensional (1D) linear scaling factors for the core and window dimensions denoted as  $\lambda_\mathrm{V}$ and $\lambda_\mathrm{I}$ respectively are derived from Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_V} and Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_I} using Eq. \ref{eq:Ac} and Eq. \ref{eq:Ac_Aw} as follows. Terms with subscript "new" represent the power level to be scaled to, wheras terms with the subscript "old" represent the original power level.
\begin{align}
    A_{\mathrm{c,new}} = (\lambda_V w_{\mathrm{core,old}})(\lambda_V l_{\mathrm{core,old}}) &= \lambda_V^2 A_{\mathrm{c,old}} \\ \lambda_V &= \sqrt{\frac{V_{\mathrm{new}}}{V_{\mathrm{old}}}} \label{eq:lambda_V} \\
    A_{\mathrm{w,new}} = (\lambda_I w_{\mathrm{win,old}})(\lambda_I l_{\mathrm{win,old}}) &= \lambda_I^2 A_{\mathrm{w,old}} \\
     \lambda_I &= \sqrt{\frac{I_{\mathrm{new}}}{I_{\mathrm{old}}}} \label{eq:lambda_I}
\end{align}

Because voltage and current are scaled equally ($V_{\mathrm{new}}/V_{\mathrm{old}} = I_{\mathrm{new}}/I_{\mathrm{old}}$), the 1D scaling factors are identical ($\lambda_V = \lambda_I$), which preserves the square window aspect ratio ($l_{\mathrm{win}}/w_{\mathrm{win}} \approx 1.00$) and constant window utilization factor $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ across all power levels.

The following can then be derived:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Core Dimensions:} The center-leg ($l_{\mathrm{core}} \times w_{\mathrm{core}}$) and outer-leg ($l_{\mathrm{core}} \times 0.5w_{\mathrm{core}}$) dimensions are scaled linearly by $\lambda_V$ as follows from Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_V}.
    \item \textbf{Window and Conductor Dimensions:} The window width $w_{\mathrm{win}}$, window height $l_{\mathrm{win}}$, wire thickness $t$, clearances $d_{\mathrm{clear}}$, TIM thickness $d_{\mathrm{TIM}}$, and inter-turn insulation distance $d_{\mathrm{inter}}$ are scaled linearly by $\lambda_I$ which follows from Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_I}.
    \item \textbf{Air Gap Length:} The air-gap $l_{\mathrm{g}}$ is adjusted to maintain a constant $L_{\mathrm{m}}$ with a constant $N_\mathrm{p}$ and changing $A_{\mathrm{c}}$. The $l_\mathrm{g}$ scales according to $\lambda_V^2$ using Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_V} and Eq. \ref{eq:Lm} in Chapter 3. 
    \begin{align}
        L_{\mathrm{m}} &= \frac{N_\mathrm{p}^2}{ \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{m, eq}} \approx \frac{N_\mathrm{p}^2}{\mathcal{R}_\mathrm{gap}} \label{eq:Lm_approx} \\
        \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{gap, old} &= \mathcal{R}_\mathrm{gap, new} \\ 
        \frac{l_{\mathrm{g,old}}}{\mu_0 \cdot A_{\mathrm{c,old}}} &= \frac{l_{\mathrm{g,new}}}{\mu_0 \cdot A_{\mathrm{c,new}}} \label{eq:Rgap_const} \\
        l_{\mathrm{g,new}} &= l_{\mathrm{g,old}} \cdot \frac{A_{\mathrm{c,new}}}{A_{\mathrm{c,old}}} = \lambda_V^2 l_{\mathrm{g,old}} \label{eq:lg_scale}
    \end{align}
    It is however noted that the fringing effect is strictly considered in the 1 kW baseline design. If fringing effects are strictly considered in the scaled-up versions, the scaling factor would be different. However, for simplicty, it is first kept as shown. 
\end{itemize}

As a result, using Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_V} and Eq. \ref{eq:lambda_I}, the total scaling factor for the $AP$ is defined as $\lambda_{AP} = \lambda_V^2 \cdot \lambda_I^2$. 

Since the physical transformer volume scales according to $Vol_{\mathrm{tr}} \propto P_{\mathrm{o}}^{3/4} \propto AP^{3/4}$, the overall three-dimensional volume of the scaled transformers increases precisely by $\lambda_{AP}^{3/4}$. Consequently, any single linear dimension scales by the cube root of the volume, equivalent to $(\lambda_{AP}^{3/4})^{1/3} = \lambda_{AP}^{1/4}$. Particularly for this case, because $\lambda_V = \lambda_I$ in this study, substituting $\lambda_I$ into the definition yields $\lambda_{AP} = \lambda_I^4 = \lambda_V^4$. Therefore, in this study $\lambda_I$ and $\lambda_V$ also equals $\lambda_{AP}^{1/4}$, which perfectly reflects the uniform three-dimensional linear expansion of the transformer geometry. The resulting physical and electrical parameters for the 1~kW, 10~kW, and 100~kW transformer models are detailed in Table~\ref{tab:power_scaling_specs}. The $L_\mathrm{m}$ values have been obtained and verified through electromagnetic simulation. To summarize the guidelines visually, Figure \ref{fig:edgewise_summary} and Figure \ref{fig:flatwise_summary} is provided.

\subsection{Simulation Conditions}
Similar to the simulation framework established in Chapter 4, the scaled flatwise-wound and edgewise-wound transformers are simulated in Ansys Electronics using the Maxwell Eddy Current and Icepak solvers. For the electromagnetic simulations at 28.5~kHz, equivalent sinusoidal RMS excitations corresponding to full-load operation in Table~\ref{tab:power_electrical_specs} are applied to each model: the primary and secondary windings are excited with 100~V rms and 10~A rms for the 1~kW models (TrE1, TrF1), 316~V rms and 31.6~A rms for the 10~kW models (TrE10, TrF10), and 1000~V rms and 100~A rms for the 100~kW models (TrE100, TrF100). Because the core cross-sectional area $A_{\mathrm{c}}$ and conductor cross-sectional area scale proportionally with voltage and current, the magnetic flux density $B_{\mathrm{m}}$ and current density $J$ distributions remain consistent with the 1~kW baseline verified in Chapter 4.

The ohmic and core losses generated in the aluminum blocks, EE-core, and copper windings from the Maxwell Eddy Current solver are then coupled directly into Ansys Icepak to evaluate the 3D steady-state temperature distribution. To provide a standardized, general representation of active cooling across all three power scales, each transformer is mounted via a bottom TIM layer (10~W/(m$\cdot$K)) onto a fixed $20^\circ\text{C}$ cold-plate boundary covering the bottom face of the transformer, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:coldplate_setup}, with the surrounding ambient temperature set at $25.0^\circ\text{C}$. This boundary condition ensures that the cooling power scales proportionally with the bottom footprint area of each transformer, guaranteeing a fair comparison across all power levels. More detailed setup conditions are presented in the Appendix: \nameref{app:ansys}.

As demonstrated in Table~\ref{tab:power_scaling_losses} and Figure~\ref{fig:1kW_scalability}, Figure~\ref{fig:10kW_scalability}, and Figure~\ref{fig:100kW_scalability}, the divergence in thermal performance between the two winding orientations becomes dramatically amplified as the power rating increases:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{At 1~kW:} With the $20^\circ\text{C}$ bottom cold plate, TrE1 reaches a peak winding temperature of $36.8^\circ\text{C}$, whereas the proposed flatwise transformer (TrF1) remains at just $21.2^\circ\text{C}$ (only $1.2^\circ\text{C}$ above the cold-plate temperature).
    \item \textbf{At 10~kW:} As total losses increase to approximately 21~W and the inter-turn clearances scale thicker by $\lambda_I = \sqrt[4]{10}$, the series thermal resistance of the edgewise winding traps heat in the upper turns, driving the peak temperature of TrE10 to $78.2^\circ\text{C}$. In stark contrast, the parallel heat conduction paths of TrF10 restrict the peak winding temperature to $24.2^\circ\text{C}$.
    \item \textbf{At 100~kW:} At full scale operation, TrE100 experiences severe entrapment of heat, reaching a peak winding temperature of $210.6^\circ\text{C}$ due to the cumulative thermal resistance of the stacked layers and the increased vertical distance from the bottom cold plate. Conversely, the proposed flatwise-wound transformer TrF100 maintains a remarkably low peak winding temperature of only $37.0^\circ\text{C}$.
\end{itemize}

Examining the electrical loss breakdown in Table~\ref{tab:power_scaling_losses}, the flatwise configuration incurs a slightly higher winding loss of $126.40\text{ W}$ at 100~kW compared to the edgewise configuration at $107.90\text{ W}$, resulting in a total loss of $166.33\text{ W}$ compared to $148.66\text{ W}$. The winding losses in both configurations increase marginally as the power increases due to the increase in thickness of the wires, eventually crossing the skin depth limit at 28.5 kHz, with $t = 0.95$ mm at 100 kW . The reason, however, as to why the flatwise-wound configuration would have more losses remains as a next area for research.

\subsection{Discussion on Conservative Scaling and Insulation Constraints}
It is important to emphasize that the scaling methodology implemented in this analysis represents a controlled, thermally conservative baseline. By scaling all linear window dimensions, including the conductor thickness $t$, clearances $d_{\mathrm{clear}}$, TIM thickness $d_{\mathrm{TIM}}$, and inter-turn distance $d_{\mathrm{inter}}$, uniformly by the 1D current scaling factor $\lambda_I$, the window utilization factor $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ and the square window aspect ratio ($l_{\mathrm{win}}/w_{\mathrm{win}} \approx 1.00$) were held strictly constant across all power levels. 

However, in actual high-voltage converter designs, maintaining strict $E$-field isolation capability requires a more complex scaling approach. As established in Chapter 3, Eq. \ref{eq:iso, min}, the minimum required insulation thickness is directly proportional to the applied voltage $d_{\mathrm{inter,min}} \propto V_{\mathrm{in}}$. In the current analysis, where $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ is constant, because voltage scales by $\lambda_V^2$ (e.g., $10\times$ at 100~kW) while window dimensions only scale by $\lambda_I = \sqrt{10}$ ($\sim 3.16\times$), the electric field stress across the inter-turn air gaps increases at higher power ratings, reaching approximately $3.16\text{ kV/mm}$ at 100~kW, right at the dielectric breakdown threshold of air. 

To maintain a constant electric field without over-stressing the insulation medium at 100~kW, a rigorous high-voltage scaling approach dictates that copper dimensions set by $t$ scale by $\lambda_I$ to preserve the current density $J$, whereas insulation and clearance distances ($d_{\mathrm{inter}}$, $d_{\mathrm{clear}}$, and $d_{\mathrm{TIM}}$) must scale proportionally to the voltage ratio $\lambda_V^2$, mirroring the air gap $l_{\mathrm{g}}$ scaling in Eq. \ref{eq:lg_scale}. Under this non-uniform scaling, the insulation layers grow faster than the conductor cross-sections with  $\lambda_V^2 > \lambda_I$, causing the window utilization factor $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ to decrease and stretching the window dimensions according to the total number of turns, $N_{\mathrm{tot}}$, the following then represent a stricter scaling condition for the window dimensions in the edgewise, represented by and flatwise-wound configurations. In this study, because $n=1$, then $N_\mathrm{tot} = 2 N_\mathrm{p}$. It also follows that the volume scaling laws may not hold according to what has been established.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Edgewise Configuration:}
    \begin{align}
        w_{\mathrm{win,new}} &= 2\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{clear}} + \lambda_I h_{\mathrm{wire}}, \\
        l_{\mathrm{win,new}} &= 2\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{TIM}} + N_{\mathrm{tot}}\lambda_I t + (N_{\mathrm{tot}} - 1)\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{inter}}
    \end{align}
    \item \textbf{Flatwise Configuration:}
    \begin{align}
        w_{\mathrm{win,new}} &= 2\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{clear}} + N_{\mathrm{tot}}\lambda_I t + (N_{\mathrm{tot}} - 1)\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{inter}} \\
        l_{\mathrm{win,new}} &= 2\lambda_V^2 d_{\mathrm{TIM}} + \lambda_I h_{\mathrm{wire}}
    \end{align}
\end{itemize}

Crucially, however, this conservative constant-$K_{\mathrm{u}}$ baseline actually heavily favors the conventional edgewise design. In the edgewise structure, heat must conduct in series across all $(N_{\mathrm{tot}} - 1)$ inter-turn insulation layers ($d_{\mathrm{inter}}$) to reach the core. If $d_{\mathrm{inter}}$ were expanded by $\lambda_V^2 = 10$ (to $1.00\text{ mm}$) at 100~kW, the vertical series thermal resistance of the edgewise winding would increase even further, driving its peak temperature significantly higher than $210.6^\circ\text{C}$. Conversely, because heat in the proposed flatwise structure flows in parallel along the copper turns directly to the top and bottom core yokes, widening the horizontal inter-turn spacing $d_{\mathrm{inter}}$ does not obstruct the primary heat extraction path. Thus, even under more complex high-voltage insulation scaling where $K_{\mathrm{u}}$ is reduced, it is expected that the thermal superiority of the flatwise-wound architecture remains robust and uncompromised. Nevertheless, this is still yet a conservative correction, as more industry standards such that of  IEC 60664 and IEC 62477 require further consideration and strict requirements for insulation coordination. This may indeed result into a larger footprint required compared to the estimation established in this scalability analyses.
